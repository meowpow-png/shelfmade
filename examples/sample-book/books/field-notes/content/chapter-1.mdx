# The Harbour at Dawn

<img
  className="chapter-image"
  src="books/field-notes/content/images/map.svg"
  alt="A sketch map of the coast"
/>

The harbour wakes before the town does. Long before the bakery lifts its shutters, the first boats are already sliding past the breakwater, their engines a low murmur under the cry of the gulls. From the end of the stone pier you can watch the whole procession: the trawlers heading for the deeper water, the small open boats making for the mussel lines, and, every so often, a single rower who seems to have no destination at all.

I came here intending to stay for a week. That was three summers ago. What kept me was not any single view or meal or conversation, but the steady rhythm of the place, a rhythm that seems to have been set by the tides long before anyone thought to write a timetable. The fishmonger opens when the first catch comes in. The ferry leaves when the water is deep enough over the bar. Even the church bell, I am told, was once rung according to the height of the water rather than the hour of the day.

## Reading the water

Learning to read the harbour took me most of that first summer. The old men who sit on the bench outside the chandlery can tell you, without looking up from their newspapers, whether the wind will turn before noon. They read it in the colour of the water near the slipway, in the way the moored boats swing on their chains, in the smell of the air coming down from the hills. When I asked one of them how he knew, he shrugged and said that he had simply been paying attention for seventy years.

<PullQuote source="Overheard outside the chandlery">
  The sea doesn't keep secrets. It just speaks slowly, and most people stop listening.
</PullQuote>

I have tried to pay the same kind of attention. Every morning I write down what I see from the pier: the direction of the wind, the number of boats out, the height of the swell against the harbour wall, the birds that are feeding and the ones that are not. Most of these notes are dull. Taken together, though, they have started to show me patterns that no single morning could reveal, and that is really what this book is about.

## The first boats

The first boat out is almost always the same one, a blue-hulled crabber with a wheelhouse no bigger than a telephone box. Her skipper is a quiet woman in her sixties who has fished these waters since she was a girl. She told me once that she leaves early not because the crabs care what time it is, but because she likes to have the sea to herself for an hour before the radio chatter begins.

By the time the sun clears the headland, the harbour is busy. Engines cough into life, ropes are thrown and caught, and someone is always shouting about a misplaced bucket. Then, just as suddenly, it is quiet again. The boats are gone, the gulls have followed them, and the water settles back into a flat, silver calm that will last until the first of them returns in the afternoon.
