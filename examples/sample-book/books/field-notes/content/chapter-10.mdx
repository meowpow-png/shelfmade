# The Lighthouse Keeper's Garden

At the far end of the coast road, where the tarmac gives way to gravel and the gravel to a narrow footpath, stands the old lighthouse. It has been automatic for decades now, its lamp controlled from an office many miles away, but the keeper's cottage beside it is still lived in, and its garden is the most remarkable I have ever seen.

The garden belongs to a retired schoolteacher who bought the cottage when the last keeper left. When she arrived, she told me, there was nothing but salt-burned grass and a single stubborn rose bush clinging to the leeward wall. Over thirty years she has turned that patch of exposed headland into something extraordinary: a walled garden of herbs, vegetables and flowers that thrives in a place where, by any reasonable judgement, nothing should grow at all.

<PullQuote>
  You don't fight the wind here. You build a wall, and then you plant something that likes walls.
</PullQuote>

## Walls and shelter

The secret, as she explained it, is shelter. Every bed in the garden is protected by a low wall of dry stone, built from rocks gathered on the beach below. The walls break the force of the wind and soak up the warmth of the sun during the day, releasing it slowly through the cool nights. Inside these small enclosures the air is still and surprisingly warm, and plants that would be flattened in minutes on the open headland grow tall and strong.

She built most of the walls herself, one stone at a time, over many summers. Some of them have fallen and been rebuilt three or four times. She showed me the oldest, a curving wall around the herb bed, and pointed out the stones she had placed in her first year. They were easy to spot: rounder, less carefully chosen, wedged into place with an enthusiasm that had not yet learned patience.

## What grows by the sea

The garden is full of plants that have made peace with salt and wind. Thyme and rosemary crowd the sunniest corners. Sea kale, with its thick, waxy leaves, spreads along the foot of the walls. There are potatoes in the sheltered beds, onions and garlic in neat rows, and a tangle of nasturtiums that spills over everything in late summer like a bright orange tide.

When I left that afternoon, she pressed a bundle of rosemary into my hands and told me to plant it somewhere sheltered. It is growing now in a pot on my windowsill, protected by the glass from the wind outside, and every time I brush against it I think of that garden at the edge of the land, built one stone at a time.
