# Weather and the Town

Every small coastal town has its own relationship with the weather, and this one is no exception. Here the weather is not something that happens to you; it is something you negotiate with. Washing goes out on the line only after a careful study of the clouds above the western hills. Picnics are planned with three alternative locations. Weddings, I am told, have been moved at an hour's notice because of a change in the wind.

<Sidebar title="Reading the clouds">
  Low, fast-moving clouds from the south-west usually mean rain within a few hours. High, thin
  streaks across a blue sky often arrive a day before a change in the weather. A clear sky with no
  wind at dusk promises a cold night and, in autumn, fog over the harbour by morning.
</Sidebar>

The most important forecast in town is not the one on the radio but the one chalked each morning on the board outside the harbour office. It is written by the harbour master, who combines the official forecast with his own observations and a healthy scepticism about both. His predictions are rarely more than a few words long, and they are almost always right.

## Storm season

Late autumn brings the storms. They arrive from the Atlantic with surprising speed, sometimes with only a few hours' warning. When the harbour master changes the board to read simply "Stay in", the whole town changes pace. Boats are doubled up on their moorings, shutters are fastened, and the shops along the front stack sandbags against their doors as calmly as if they were putting out the morning's newspapers.

I spent my first storm in the back room of the pub, along with half the town. The power failed at about nine o'clock, and for the rest of the evening we sat by candlelight while the wind roared down the chimney and the windows rattled in their frames. Nobody seemed particularly worried. Someone produced a fiddle, someone else produced a remarkably large flask of soup, and by midnight the storm had become just another excuse for a gathering.

## After the storm

The morning after a storm has a particular kind of beauty. The air is scrubbed clean, the light is sharp and bright, and the beach is covered with treasures thrown up by the waves: driftwood, tangled nets, the occasional glass float, and once, memorably, an entire wooden door. Children are sent out early with buckets to collect whatever they can find, and the most interesting objects end up on display in the window of the harbour office, each with a small handwritten label explaining where it was found.

It is on these mornings that I most understand why people stay here. The storms are hard, and the winters are long, but there is something deeply reassuring about a place that has learned, over centuries, to bend without breaking.
